2. Understand the system before wiring

The critical rule is: Gate B must not be commanded open until Gate A is confirmed closed and the chamber check is acceptable. A valid RFID card and PIN authorize one passage; they do not bypass the physical chamber checks.

Normal passage:
Authenticate → A opens → entry detected → stable expected load → A closes → A‑CLOSED switch active → chamber acceptable → B opens → exit detected → chamber becomes empty → B closes.
Possible tailgating / uncertainty:
Repeated IR‑A activity, long blockage, unusually high/unstable load, contradictory sensors, or gate fault → keep B closed → alarm → safely reopen A if needed.

2A. Simplest final build path

The final system includes the ESP32‑AI‑CAM. To keep debugging manageable, assemble it in layers, but do not treat the ESP32 as an optional feature.

StageWhat you buildWhy
Stage 1 — Core electronicsUNO, LCD, PCF8574 + keypad, RC522 + CD4050, HX711 + 1 kg load cell, 2 IR sensors, 2 servos, 1 Gate-A-closed switch, emergency button, buzzerProves the safety/interlock logic before networking is added.
Stage 2 — ESP32‑AI‑CAMProgram and test camera + Wi‑Fi separately, then connect its UART to the UNO using A2/A3 and the CD4050.Keeps camera/network debugging separate from gate debugging.
Stage 3 — Final integrationUNO sends event messages to ESP32; ESP32 can capture a photo, maintain a web-status page, and optionally send Telegram/network notifications.Final demonstration has both physical security logic and connected monitoring.
Final architecture: the UNO is always the primary safety controller. The ESP32‑AI‑CAM is included and active, but it never gets authority to open Gate B. It receives event/status messages from the UNO and handles camera + Wi‑Fi functions.

Complexity reductions retained

  • No RTC and no microSD: these are intentionally omitted from this build.
  • No separate power-distribution board: dedicated breadboard rails are used.
  • No fuse required to make the prototype work: the missing fuse holder remains unused until a proper fuse is available.
  • One limit switch for now: place it at Gate A CLOSED, the most important interlock verification.
  • No AI/ML: “AI‑Thinker” is the board/module name. We use the ESP32‑CAM only for image capture, Wi‑Fi, web status and notifications.
  • PCF8574 keypad scanning uses Wire directly: this avoids an extra keypad-I²C library.
  • Use one or two hard-coded RFID UID + PIN pairs: no external database is needed.
  • Third FC‑51 remains a spare: only IR‑A and IR‑B are installed.

4. Power wiring — no separate power rail and no fuse required to start

Important clarification: when this guide says servo power rail, it does not mean a separate product. A pair of unused red/blue side strips on your breadboard can be the servo power rail. You already have what you need.

The easiest current arrangement uses two electrically separate 5 V supplies:

4.1 Choose two different breadboard rail pairs

Most full-size breadboards have long side rails marked + and −. Use one pair for normal Arduino logic and, if your breadboard has another unused pair, use that second pair for the servos. Put a small label or tape on them:

LOGIC +5VFrom UNO 5V. LCD, PCF8574, HX711, FC-51 and suitable buzzer module use this.
LOGIC GNDFrom UNO GND. All logic devices use this ground.
SERVO +5V / GNDFrom the separate regulated 5V source. Only servos and the 2200µF capacitor use the positive servo rail.
Check whether your breadboard rails are split in the middle. Many breadboards have a hidden break halfway along a red/blue rail. With power OFF, use multimeter continuity mode to test the two ends. If they are split and you need the whole length, bridge only the matching halves with a short jumper: + to + and − to −.

4.2 Connect the logic supply

1
Leave every sensor/module disconnected. Connect the power bank to the UNO using USB.
2
UNO 5V → LOGIC + rail. UNO GND → LOGIC GND rail.
3
Set the multimeter to DC voltage. Put black probe on LOGIC GND and red probe on LOGIC +. Confirm approximately 5 V.

4.3 Make the separate servo supply on the breadboard

1
With the servo battery/source disconnected, choose a second unused +/− rail pair and label it SERVO.
2
Recommended: connect battery/source positive → one identified pole of the DPST rocker switch, then from its switched output to the SERVO + rail. The switch is optional; if you want the absolute simplest wiring, battery/source positive may go directly to the SERVO + rail.
3
Connect battery/source negative directly → SERVO GND rail.
4
Connect the 2200 µF capacitor across the SERVO rails: capacitor + to SERVO +5V and the striped − lead to SERVO GND.
5
Before inserting either servo, turn the switch ON and measure between SERVO + and SERVO GND. Confirm approximately 5 V, then switch it OFF again.

4.4 Make the common ground

1
Run one jumper from SERVO GND rail to LOGIC GND rail. This is the common ground connection.
2
With power OFF, use continuity mode between the two GND rails. It should beep. Test between the two positive rails: they should not have continuity.

4.5 What about the missing fuse?

The circuit still works without a fuse. A fuse is protection against excessive current or a short circuit; it is not required for the Arduino logic to function. Since the actual fuse was not delivered, leave the fuse holder completely out of this first circuit.

Current simple path:

5V battery + → rocker switch → SERVO + breadboard rail
5V battery - → SERVO GND breadboard rail
Do not put a piece of wire, foil, or another conductor into the fuse holder to imitate a fuse. Simply do not use the holder until a proper fuse is obtained.

4.6 Breadboard servo-power limitations

For this small tabletop prototype, using a dedicated breadboard rail for two lightly loaded SG90 servos is the simplest approach. Keep the gates very light, wires short, and do not stall the servos against the cardboard. If the UNO resets, the rail voltage drops badly, the breadboard gets warm, or servo motion is erratic, stop and move the high-current servo distribution to thicker direct wiring/screw terminals.

Your red/black cable and screw terminals can be used later for a more robust version, but they are not required for the first successful build.

4A. Every small purchased item — exactly where it goes and whether you must use it

1) 0.1 µF ceramic capacitor — RECOMMENDED

Purpose: local noise suppression/decoupling for the CD4050. It supplies a tiny local energy reserve when logic transitions occur.

Polarity: ceramic capacitor is not polarized; either leg can go to either side.

UNO 3.3V ───── CD4050 pin 1 (VCC) │ 0.1µF │ GND ─────────── CD4050 pin 8 (VSS)

Placement: put it physically close to CD4050 pins 1 and 8 on the breadboard.

Can you skip it? The circuit may still run without it, but do not skip it in your final build because you already have the part and it improves reliability.

2) 2200 µF electrolytic capacitor — RECOMMENDED

Purpose: helps the servo rail tolerate short current surges when SG90 motors start or change direction.

Polarity: this capacitor is polarized. The negative side normally has a stripe. Reverse polarity is wrong.

SERVO +5V rail ───────────┬──── Servo A red ├──── Servo B red │ (+) 2200µF (-) │ SERVO GND rail ───────────┴──── Servo grounds

Where: plug it across the dedicated servo breadboard rails, preferably close to where both servos connect.

Can you skip it? Yes, the logic does not depend on it, but servo-related resets/jitter become more likely. Use it.

3) CD4050 — REQUIRED

Purpose: the UNO uses 5 V digital logic while RC522/ESP32 inputs are 3.3 V-class. The CD4050 lowers UNO output logic levels before those signals reach the 3.3 V devices.

Power: CD4050 pin 1 → UNO 3.3 V; pin 8 → GND; 0.1 µF capacitor across pins 1 and 8.

ChannelUNO input to CD4050CD4050 outputDestination
1D10 → pin 3pin 2RC522 SDA/SS
2D11 → pin 5pin 4RC522 MOSI
3D13 → pin 7pin 6RC522 SCK
4A0 → pin 9pin 10RC522 RST

Important: RC522 MISO → UNO D12 directly; ESP32 TX → UNO A2 directly. Those directions already originate from 3.3 V devices.

4) BC547 transistor — OPTIONAL

Use it only if: your buzzer is a simple bare 2-pin active buzzer and you want the transistor to switch its current.

Do not use it if: your buzzer is a ready-made 3-pin module with VCC, GND and SIG/S pins. In that case connect the module directly as described in the buzzer section.

Typical transistor-driver concept:

5V ───────────── buzzer + buzzer - ────── BC547 collector BC547 emitter ─ GND UNO D9 ─ resistor ─ BC547 base
Your final receipt shows 10 kΩ resistors, not a 1 kΩ–2.2 kΩ base resistor. Therefore, to reduce complexity, use a 3-pin active buzzer module if that is what you already own and leave both BC547 transistors unused. Do not build the transistor stage just because you bought the transistors.

Pin-order warning: BC547 lead order can be misread when rotated. Check the exact package/datasheet or test the part before wiring; do not rely on a generic drawing without orientation.

5) 10 kΩ resistors — OPTIONAL / SPARE

They are not needed for:

  • Emergency button — UNO D2 uses INPUT_PULLUP.
  • Gate A closed switch — UNO A1 uses INPUT_PULLUP.
  • RC522 level conversion — CD4050 already does it.
  • ESP32 RX level conversion — CD4050 already does it.

Keep them as spare general pull-up/pull-down resistors. If later you add LEDs, use proper LED current-limiting resistors rather than assuming 10 kΩ is appropriate.

6) DPST rocker switch — OPTIONAL BUT USEFUL

The simplest use is as a master ON/OFF switch for the separate servo supply. You may use only one pole of the DPST device.

5V servo source (+) ── rocker switch ── SERVO + breadboard rail 5V servo source (-) ───────────────── SERVO GND breadboard rail

If you skip it: connect the servo source directly to the servo rails and disconnect the source manually when you want the servos off.

Before wiring: use the multimeter continuity mode to identify which two rocker terminals become connected in the ON position. Do not guess from physical appearance.

7) Fuse + fuse holder — NOT REQUIRED TO FUNCTION

Purpose: electrical protection if the servo-power wiring shorts or draws excessive current.

Current situation: you received the holder but not the fuse. The project still works. Leave the holder out completely.

CURRENT BUILD: 5V servo source + ── rocker switch ── SERVO + rail LATER, WITH CORRECT FUSE: 5V servo source + ── fuse holder + fuse ── rocker ── SERVO + rail
Never replace a missing fuse with wire, foil, a resistor, or any improvised conductor. An empty holder is simply not used.

Is it mandatory? No for the tabletop prototype's operation. Recommended as a later safety improvement.

8) DC barrel jack 5.5×2.1 mm female adapter — OPTIONAL

Use it only when your regulated 5 V source ends in a matching 5.5×2.1 mm male barrel plug.

5V source with male barrel plug │ ▼ female barrel-jack adapter + terminal ── rocker ── SERVO + rail - terminal ──────────── SERVO GND rail

If the battery/source already gives you bare + and − wires: skip the barrel jack entirely.

Do not assume barrel polarity. Many supplies are center-positive, but verify the adapter's +/− markings and confirm output polarity with the multimeter before connecting the servo rail.

9) Micro‑USB power jack/breakout — OPTIONAL

This is only a convenience part for exposing 5 V and GND from a USB power cable/power bank.

Possible use in this project:

Power bank USB │ micro‑USB breakout ├── 5V ─────> ESP32‑CAM 5V └── GND ────> common GND

Before using it: identify the board's actual VBUS/5V and GND labels and measure the output with the multimeter. Do not connect a data pin by mistake.

Do not use it for the two servos unless you know the breakout/cable can safely carry the required current. The simpler plan is still the separate regulated servo source.

If ESP32 already has a stable 5 V connection: leave this breakout unused.

10) 2-pin screw-terminal blocks — OPTIONAL

These are mainly for making power wires removable and secure in a more permanent build.

Examples:

  • Servo source +/− entering a perfboard.
  • Load-cell cable entering a small interface board.
  • External power input.

If your terminal blocks are PCB-mount types, they normally need to be soldered to a board. They are therefore not mandatory in your solderless breadboard version. Do not try to force thick terminal pins into breadboard holes.

11) Red/black power wire — RECOMMENDED

Use red for +5 V and black for GND where possible, especially around the separate servo supply. This reduces polarity mistakes and is often more robust than thin Dupont wires for power.

It is not mandatory if your available jumper wires are short, secure and remain cool during servo operation.

12) Multimeter — STRONGLY RECOMMENDED TOOL

It is not physically installed in the project. Use it before connecting sensitive modules:

  • Measure LOGIC rail ≈5 V.
  • Measure CD4050 / RC522 rail ≈3.3 V.
  • Measure SERVO rail ≈5 V.
  • Check the two +5 V supplies are not tied together.
  • Check common-ground continuity.
  • Identify rocker-switch terminals.
  • Identify emergency-button contacts.
  • Test Gate A limit-switch COM/NO behavior.
  • Check barrel-jack polarity if used.
Recommended low-complexity choice: actually install CD4050, both capacitors and the rocker switch. Leave the fuse holder, barrel jack, micro‑USB breakout, screw-terminal blocks, BC547s and 10 kΩ resistors unused unless a specific need arises. This gives the cleanest beginner build with the parts you already own.

9. Build the 1 kg load-cell + HX711 chamber floor from scratch

What this subsystem does: the load cell does not directly tell us “one person” or “two people.” It measures how much force/weight is on the chamber floor. The UNO combines that result with IR‑A, IR‑B and gate state. An abnormal value becomes Possible Tailgating or Unexpected Chamber Condition.

9.1 Understand the two parts

Your ElectronicsBD 1 kg sensor is a 4-wire strain-gauge load cell. When the aluminum bar bends slightly, internal strain gauges change resistance and produce a very small analog differential voltage. That signal is too small for the UNO to read directly. The HX711 is the amplifier + 24-bit ADC that converts it into a digital number the UNO can read.

9.5 First raw-reading test

Install an HX711 library, connect only the load subsystem, and print raw values. The purpose is not yet to obtain grams; simply verify that adding weight changes the reading in a repeatable direction.

#include <HX711.h>

HX711 scale;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(9600);
  scale.begin(3, 4);   // DOUT=D3, SCK=D4
}

void loop() {
  if (scale.is_ready()) {
    long raw = scale.read_average(10);
    Serial.println(raw);
  } else {
    Serial.println("HX711 not ready");
  }
  delay(300);
}

With the empty platform, the number should remain in roughly the same region. Put a small object on the platform: the value should change significantly. Remove it: the value should return close to the original region.

If the value moves “negative” when weight is added: this is not necessarily a fault. You can swap A+ and A− or simply use a negative calibration factor later.

9.6 Tare: make the empty chamber equal zero

The platform itself has weight. We do not want that counted as an intruder. At startup, when the chamber is confirmed empty, call scale.tare(). That stores the current empty reading as zero.

scale.begin(3, 4);
delay(500);
scale.tare();       // chamber platform must be EMPTY here
Do not tare while an object/model is standing in the chamber. That would make the occupied chamber appear to be zero.

9.7 Calibrate it into grams

  1. Finish the physical platform first.
  2. Power the system and tare the empty platform.
  3. Place a known weight on the center of the platform — for example an accurately known 100 g or 200 g object.
  4. Read the raw change from zero.
  5. Adjust the calibration factor until scale.get_units(10) reports approximately that known weight.
  6. Repeat with the object removed and replaced several times.

The final code will keep a configuration value such as:

float LOAD_CALIBRATION_FACTOR = -1234.5;   // EXAMPLE ONLY

Your real factor will be determined experimentally. Do not copy an Internet calibration factor because every load cell, HX711 and mechanical installation is different.

15.4 Power the ESP32‑AI‑CAM

The ESP32‑CAM board itself normally accepts 5 V at its 5V pin and performs onboard regulation. Do not attempt to run the camera board from the UNO 3.3 V pin.

Preferred order:

  1. Best: a separate stable 5 V branch from the power bank/source for the ESP32‑CAM, with common ground.
  2. Acceptable prototype test: if your power-bank/UNO logic supply is strong and stable, ESP32 5V may share the logic 5 V source. Watch for brownout/restarts.
  3. Avoid: powering ESP32‑CAM from the noisy servo +5 V breadboard rail unless no other option exists.
If the ESP32 repeatedly prints brownout/reset messages, freezes when taking a photo, or resets when Wi‑Fi transmits, treat that as a power problem first.

Do not classify weight as “two people detected.” Weight is only one clue. Use the wording Possible Tailgating or Unexpected Chamber Condition.

18. Calibration procedure

A. Servo angles

Find four software angles: A_CLOSED_ANGLE, A_OPEN_ANGLE, B_CLOSED_ANGLE, B_OPEN_ANGLE. Start conservatively and adjust in 2–5° increments. The cardboard gate should reach its end position without the servo buzzing continuously.

B. Gate A closed switch

Adjust the switch position until it activates only at the final closed position. After 20 repeated open/close cycles, A1 should report closed every time.

C. IR sensors

Test with the actual model/object used for the presentation. Set each FC‑51 sensitivity so the opposite wall does not cause a permanent detection.

D. Load cell

First record the empty platform reading and tare it to zero. Then place one known demonstration object and record its stable weight. Repeat at least 10 times. Your “acceptable chamber” range should be wide enough to tolerate noise but narrow enough to notice a clearly heavier second object.

Example numbers such as 250 g for one model and 500 g for two are only examples. Use your measured values. Do not hard-code an arbitrary threshold before calibration.

E. Timing

Measure how long a normal model takes to enter and exit. Set timeouts comfortably above normal passage, but not so long that a stuck sequence persists indefinitely.

20. Optional future improvements — not required for this version

More gate-position switches.
Your current design uses the one available switch for Gate A CLOSED. Three additional switches could later verify A‑OPEN, B‑OPEN and B‑CLOSED, but they are not required to build the current simplified prototype.
Fuse.
The holder can remain unused. When the correct 5×20 mm fuse is available and its rating is chosen for your servo-power source, place it in series with servo-source positive before the rocker switch.
Permanent wiring.
After the breadboard version works reliably, you may move only the power distribution and mechanically weak connections to perfboard, screw terminals or soldered joints. This is an optional reliability upgrade.
RTC and microSD are not part of this project version. Do not add them unless you later decide you want local timestamped logging.

Reference notes used for the final wiring choices

The ElectronicsBD listing for the purchased 1 kg load cell identifies it as a 4-wire device and lists Red=VCC, Black=GND, White=Output+, Green=Output−; the seller also recommends the HX711. Their HX711 listing identifies the module as a 5 V, 24-bit ADC/amplifier suitable for 4-wire differential load cells. Espressif's current Arduino-ESP32 documentation recommends installing the ESP32 platform through Boards Manager, and the official CameraWebServer source includes a dedicated CAMERA_MODEL_AI_THINKER pin mapping. These references are why this guide uses the stated load-cell/HX711 wiring and the official AI-Thinker camera model instead of manually assigning camera pins.
